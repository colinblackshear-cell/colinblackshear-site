What happens when we die

Every tradition draws a map of the same doorway.

The Bardo Thödol is one map. This atlas lays twenty-five others beside it: what each says continues, what happens at the moment of death, how long the in-between lasts, who or what judges, and where the road ends.

Is this a crazy idea? No. Several of these traditions already wrote the book.

The Tibetan text is part of a long line of guides written for the dying and the people sitting with them:

  • Coming Forth by Day, Egypt, c. 1550 BCE
  • The Orphic gold leaves, Greece, 4th c. BCE
  • The Garuda Purana, India, 1st millennium CE
  • Genshin's Ōjōyōshū, Japan, 985
  • The Ars Moriendi, Europe, c. 1415
  • Maavar Yabok, Italy, 1626

The name "Tibetan Book of the Dead" was itself borrowed from the Egyptian book by its 1927 translator. A series that sets them side by side is following a path that's already there.

Who holds these views

Most of the world expects a judgment and a resurrection

Christians and Muslims together are a little over half of humanity, and both expect each person to be judged and the dead to rise. Hindus and Buddhists, about one in five people, expect rebirth instead.

People by religion, 2020, in billionsPew Research Center, 2025

Being unaffiliated doesn't mean not believing in an afterlife. Many unaffiliated people believe in some kind of afterlife, and Pew found that 73% of all U.S. adults believe in heaven and 62% in hell (2021). "Other" includes Sikhs, Jains, Bahá'ís, Daoists, Shinto, folk and Indigenous religions.

The in-between, counted

How long does the passage take?

Many traditions give the journey a fixed number of days, and the living keep rites on that schedule. The Tibetan 49 sits in the middle of a crowded range. Gridlines fall every 7 days.

Days between death and arrival (or the end of the key rites)Hover or tap a bar for what the count marks

A ring at zero means rebirth or release is instant. Broken bars run off the scale. Islam, Christianity, Bahá'í and most others have an open-ended wait until a final judgment, so they have no bar.

The books

Six books built like the Tibetan one

Each now has a narrated manual and a daily walk. They were chosen by how well each fits the format: a source text that speaks to the dead or dying, a journey with clear stages, and a day count that turns into a daily walk. Each gets a manual plus a walk, the same pair as the Bardo series.

About these summaries

These are short, plain-language summaries of mainstream positions, drawn from each tradition's own core texts. Living traditions should be checked with practitioners before a full book is published, and Indigenous traditions in particular should only be built with the communities they belong to.

"Days" mean the count the tradition itself uses. Some are counts of the soul's journey (Tibetan, Zoroastrian, Aztec) and some are counts of mourning rites that track it (Jewish, Hindu, Orthodox).
